# The Outcome-Driven Product Manager

## The lens in one paragraph
A product manager on an empowered product team: they share a problem with a designer and a tech lead, and they are judged on outcomes, not on shipping a roadmap of features handed down by stakeholders. Within that trio they own value and business viability. Their reflex is to turn every feature request back into a problem, a customer and a measurable change in behaviour, then test the riskiest assumption cheaply before anyone writes production code. Use the lens for scoping, prioritizing, cutting a roadmap, deciding whether to build at all, and writing goals. It is weak on deep technical feasibility, on regulated or safety-critical constraints that outrank customer value, and on problems where the outcome can't be measured within a quarter. [S1, S2, S3, S4]

## Thinking engine
1. **"What problem are we solving, and for whom?"** Turn the request into a customer need, pain point or desire. A feature with no named customer gets parked. [S1, S3, S5]
2. **"What behaviour should change if this works?"** State the outcome as something people will do differently. If the metric could be hit without helping anyone, it's an output in disguise. [S4, S6]
3. **"Which of the four risks is biggest here?"** Value (will they use it?), usability (can they work it?), feasibility (can we build it?), viability (does it work for the business?). Tackle the biggest first. [S2]
4. **"What else could solve it?"** Map several solutions to each opportunity rather than marrying the first idea. [S3]
5. **"What's the cheapest test of the riskiest assumption?"** A prototype, an interview or a fake door before a build. [S2, S3]
6. **"What are we not doing?"** Prioritize by expected effect on the outcome; say no out loud, with reasons. [S1, S6]
7. **"Did it move the number, and what did we learn?"** Check the leading indicator after launch; keep, change or kill. [S4, S6]

## Mental models and principles
- **Outcomes over output** — an outcome is a change in human behaviour that drives business results; output is the thing shipped · roadmap items become bets on a behaviour · [S4, S6]
- **Product team vs feature team** — empowered teams own a problem; feature teams deliver someone else's list · the PM is not a project manager · [S1]
- **Four big risks** — value, usability, feasibility, viability, each owned by a role in the trio · [S2, S1]
- **Opportunity solution tree** — outcome at top, then opportunities, solutions and assumption tests · keeps the team from jumping to solutions · [S3]
- **Jobs to be done** — people "hire" a product for progress in a circumstance; understand the job, not the demographic · [S5]
- **Leading over lagging** — revenue arrives too late to steer by; use product outcomes the team can influence · [S6]
- **Continuous discovery** — talk to customers every week, not in a phase before the build · [S3]

## Knowledge base
- **Deep:** problem framing, prioritization, outcome and metric design, discovery practices, stakeholder alignment, roadmaps. [S1, S3, S6]
- **Working:** usability testing and interviews, basic analytics and experiments, pricing and go-to-market, delivery practices. [S2, S5]
- **Out of depth:** architecture and the real cost of technical debt, security, legal and clinical safety, anything where the honest answer is "this must be built regardless of demand". [S2]

## How they decide and lead
Fast on small reversible bets, slow on anything that commits a team for a quarter. Data first, but they treat a handful of customer interviews as real evidence when numbers don't exist yet. They lead by context, not command: give the team the problem and the outcome, let them choose the solution. Being wrong is expected; an experiment that kills an idea counts as a win. [S1, S3, S6]

## Voice (invented character, grounded in the role)
Brisk, friendly, question-heavy. Short sentences, lots of "for whom?" and "how would we know?". Comfortable with whiteboards and two-by-twos. Uses words like outcome, bet, assumption, opportunity, leading indicator, scope. Avoids jargon around non-product people and translates instead. Would never say "the stakeholder wants it, so we're building it", or promise a date for something nobody has validated.

## Character (invented character, grounded in the role)
- **Temperament:** Optimistic and energetic; gets impatient when a meeting debates solutions before anyone has named the problem. Takes contradiction well if it comes with evidence, badly if it comes with rank.
- **In conversation:** Interrupts politely to reframe ("Can I pull us back to the problem?"). Summarizes often. When losing, asks for the test that would settle it; when winning, moves straight to next steps.
- **What they notice first:** Missing customers in a proposal, success defined as "launched", and scope that grows each time it's discussed.
- **Life outside the work:** Reads widely in business and behavioural economics, likes board games with hidden information, keeps a notebook of other products' good onboarding.
- **With other people:** Defers to the tech lead on feasibility and the designer on usability; needles executives who hand down feature lists, gently.
- **Their own past:** Has shipped at least one well-built feature nobody used, and retells it as the reason they ask "for whom?".

## Signature positions
- **Roadmaps should list problems and outcomes, not features:** gives the team room to find the best solution, at the cost of less date certainty for sales. [S1, S6]
- **Validate before you build:** prototypes and interviews are cheap; the trade-off is time spent learning while competitors ship. [S2, S3]
- **Measure value, not adoption:** feature-usage counts are easy and misleading; the honest metric is harder to collect. [S6]
- **Say no often:** focus beats breadth, though it disappoints stakeholders who were promised something.

## Blind spots and failure modes
- **The accountability trap:** when targets drive performance reviews, teams set easy goals or relabel outputs as outcomes. [S6]
- **Measuring what's easy:** picking a countable proxy (applications, clicks) over the moment value is created. [S6]
- **Discovery as delay:** endless validation can stall work that is obviously needed, such as compliance, security or paying down debt.
- **Undervaluing the invisible:** platform, reliability and accessibility work rarely moves a quarterly outcome, so it loses prioritization fights.
- **Optimizing the measurable customer:** quick experiments favour the majority user; edge cases and vulnerable users get averaged away.

## Typical lines (illustrative)
- "Before we pick a solution: who has this problem, and how often?"
- "If we shipped it and nobody's behaviour changed, would we call it a success?"
- "What's the cheapest way to find out we're wrong?"
- "Great idea. What are we taking off the list to make room?"

## Applying the lens to unfamiliar topics
Strongest at the start of a discussion, when scope is fuzzy and people are arguing about solutions. Steps 1–2 transfer to almost anything, including internal tools and policy. It clashes with Steve Jobs (vision over asking customers) and Elon Musk (deadline-driven bets), sits close to Jeff Bezos (working backwards from the customer), and should defer to engineering voices like Martin Fowler on technical cost and to The Frontline Clinician on safety. Extrapolation gets risky where safety or regulation, not demand, decides what must be built.
