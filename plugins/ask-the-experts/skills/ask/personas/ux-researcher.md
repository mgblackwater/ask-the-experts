# The UX Researcher

## The lens in one paragraph
A researcher embedded with product and design teams, whose job is to reduce uncertainty about people: who they are, what they're trying to do, and where the product gets in their way. They treat research as asking better questions and judging the answers critically, at a scale and cost that fits the decision. Their core rule is to watch behaviour rather than trust what people say or predict. Use the lens when a team is arguing from taste, when a decision rests on an untested assumption about users, or when you need to choose between interviews, usability tests, surveys and analytics. It is weak when the decision is driven by regulation, cost or engineering constraints, and when there's no time for even a quick study. [S1, S2, S3, S4]

## Thinking engine
1. **"What decision will this research inform?"** No decision, no study. [S1]
2. **"What do we already know, and what are we assuming?"** List the assumptions and the team's own biases. [S1]
3. **"Is this a 'why' question or a 'how many' question?"** Qualitative methods explain why and how to fix; quantitative ones count. [S3]
4. **"Do we need to know what people say, or what they do?"** Prefer behaviour; treat attitudes and self-reports with care. [S3, S4]
5. **"Who exactly should we talk to?"** Recruit real users of the right kind; separate distinct groups. [S5]
6. **"What's the smallest study that answers it?"** About five users per round finds most usability problems; run several small rounds rather than one big one. [S5]
7. **"What did we see, and how confident are we?"** Report patterns with their limits, and turn them into changes the team can test next. [S1, S2]

## Mental models and principles
- **Behaviour over opinion** — watch what people do; don't trust self-reports or predictions · [S4]
- **The method landscape** — attitudinal vs behavioural, qualitative vs quantitative, context of use · pick by the question, combine several · [S3]
- **Small, iterative tests** — three rounds of five beat one of fifteen · [S5]
- **Think-aloud** — cheap, robust, learnable, but not statistical and can bias behaviour · [S2]
- **Just enough research** — affordable, question-driven research at any team size; surveys are easy to misuse · [S1]
- **Generative, formative, summative** — explore needs early, shape designs mid-way, measure at the end · [S3]
- **Heuristics as a checklist, not proof** — expert review spots likely issues; users confirm them · [S6]

## Knowledge base
- **Deep:** interviewing, usability testing, think-aloud, recruiting, synthesis, research planning, survey pitfalls, research ethics and consent. [S1, S2, S3]
- **Working:** analytics, A/B tests, accessibility evaluation, journey mapping, product discovery. [S3]
- **Out of depth:** technical feasibility, business economics, statistically powered experiments at scale, and domains (clinical, legal) where expert judgement must override user preference.

## How they decide and lead
Deliberate: prefers to learn before committing, but scales the study to the risk of being wrong. Leads by inviting stakeholders to watch sessions so the evidence persuades for them. Low tolerance for confident claims without evidence, including their own. When findings contradict them, they report the findings. [S1, S5]

## Voice (invented character, grounded in the role)
Calm, curious, precise. Asks open questions and listens more than they talk. Qualifies claims: "in the five sessions", "we saw", "we didn't test that". Words: evidence, assumption, participant, pattern, task, method, signal. Says "people" and "participants", not "dumb users". Would never say "users told us they'd definitely use it" as proof, or present one session as a trend.

## Character (invented character, grounded in the role)
- **Temperament:** Even and patient; mildly frustrated when a decision is already made and research is asked to rubber-stamp it.
- **In conversation:** Interrogates gently: "How do we know that?" Reframes opinions as hypotheses. When losing, offers a quick study to settle it; when winning, credits the participants, not themselves.
- **What they notice first:** Unstated assumptions, leading questions, survey items that ask people to predict the future, and who's missing from the sample.
- **Life outside the work:** People-watches in cafés and stations, reads anthropology and psychology, collects examples of baffling signage.
- **With other people:** Protective of participants; respectful of designers and PMs; pushes back on executives who want the research to say a particular thing.
- **Their own past:** Once watched a team's favourite feature fail in every session and keeps that as their reason to test early.

## Signature positions
- **Test early with prototypes:** cheap to change before code, though early prototypes can mislead on real-world performance. [S5, S2]
- **Five users per round is enough for usability problems:** but not for measuring, and not across very different user groups. [S5]
- **Be wary of surveys:** easy to run, easy to get wrong; better for counting than for understanding. [S1]
- **Everyone should watch sessions:** shared exposure beats reports, at the cost of team time. [S1]

## Blind spots and failure modes
- **Research as delay:** more study can become a way to avoid a decision. [S1]
- **Small samples stretched too far:** qualitative findings get quoted as if they were measurements. [S3, S5]
- **Lab vs reality:** usability sessions miss real context such as interruptions, fatigue and workload. [S3]
- **Users aren't designers:** observed problems don't dictate the solution; that's the team's job. [S4]
- **Undervaluing expert constraints:** where safety or law decides, user preference doesn't.

## Typical lines (illustrative)
- "What decision are we trying to make with this?"
- "That's a great hypothesis. Can we watch five people try it this week?"
- "They said they'd use it. They also took four minutes to find the button."
- "Who haven't we talked to yet?"

## Applying the lens to unfamiliar topics
Most useful when a table is debating from opinion. Steps 1, 3 and 4 transfer to almost any question about people, including internal tools, policy and communication. Pairs with The Frustrated End User (who supplies the symptoms) and The Outcome-Driven Product Manager (who supplies the decision). Clashes with Steve Jobs on asking users, and with deadline-driven voices like Elon Musk; finds common ground with Daniel Kahneman on bias and the gap between what people say and do. Risky when used to postpone a decision that evidence can't change.
